Impulse — internally titled The Bridge — is a single-page web application that turns a question usually treated as a matter of taste into a structured, scored self-assessment: not which is better, love marriage or arranged marriage, but which one your particular psychology is built to survive when it eventually fails you. This report sets out what the project is trying to do, where it sits relative to adjacent fields, how its engine actually works, what a hands-on technical review turned up, and what's changed since. Most of what was code-fixable has since been fixed — §6 and §8 carry the ledger; what remains needs the owner's evidence or a content decision, not more code.

01 / 09

Objective

Every existing "love vs. arranged" quiz on the internet asks a shallow version of the question: which do you prefer? Preference is cheap — it shifts with the last film someone watched or the last wedding they attended, and a quiz built on preference can always be flattered or gamed. Impulse's stated objective is to replace that question with a harder one, quoted directly from its own design document:

Both doors lead to a room where something breaks. Which breakage can you live inside? Impulse — design.md, §0

Concretely, the system commits to four objectives that most tools in this space skip:

  • Score fit, not preference. Twenty-six narrative choices move eight named psychological traits, which are then weighted into three comparative "doors" rather than a single yes/no.
  • Name the cost of the answer. Every door — love, arranged, or the hybrid "Bridge" — ships with a stated failure mode and an "invoice," on the explicit principle that a reading which only lists benefits is flattery, not analysis.
  • Separate fit from readiness. A person can have a textbook love-marriage temperament and be nowhere near ready to marry anyone. Impulse tracks readiness as an orthogonal ninth number that can override the entire verdict.
  • Measure inherited pressure instead of assuming it. Rather than asking "do you believe men should propose," the system asks the same six scenarios twice — what the player wants, and what they believe is expected of their gender — and scores the gap, never the content.
02 / 09

Vision & where this project belongs

Impulse doesn't sit cleanly inside one existing category. It borrows from four adjacent fields and welds them into something that reads, alternately, like a psychometric instrument, a piece of interactive fiction, and a small explainable-AI case study — despite containing no machine learning at all.

APersonality & psychometric assessment

The eight-thread model (Self-Authorship, Kinship Pull, Anxious/Avoidant Wiring, Ambiguity Tolerance, Regret Orientation, Inherited Pressure, Readiness) sits in the same family as MBTI, Big Five, or attachment-style quizzes — but scored against a real, binding life decision rather than a personality label, and explicitly disclaiming predictive power at the end of every reading.

BRelationship decision support

In spirit it's closer to a premarital-counselling instrument (the kind a therapist runs a couple through) than a viral quiz — narrative scenario, honest trade-off, a "what to actually ask for in a partner" section — except self-administered, free, and taken alone rather than as a couple.

CInteractive nonfiction

Structurally, the five chapters, the animated "two threads of light" that braid together as the player answers, and the second-person "ten years from now" framing in the final chapter put this closer to literary interactive fiction than to a form. The scoring is real, but the delivery is written, paced, and staged like a short story about the reader.

DExplainable, multi-agent reasoning

The most distinctive architectural choice is "the Council" — five specialist functions plus a router and a synthesiser, styled explicitly in the language of a multi-agent system (agents are seated or benched, they can dissent, one holds a hard veto). Nothing here is a model; every agent is a short deterministic function. But the framing borrows the ethos of explainable AI — show the arithmetic, let sub-scores disagree in front of the reader, never collapse disagreement into a single silent number — and applies it somewhere almost nothing else in this genre bothers to.

Read together, the project's vision is a tool that is honest rather than flattering: no verdict is ever framed as advice, every door has a stated price, and the system refuses to encode gender content itself — the player supplies their own expectation, and the engine only measures the distance between that and what they actually want. Its natural audience is the South Asian / diaspora reader for whom this decision is real and high-stakes, and its natural next form — if the scoring is ever validated against real outcomes — is a legitimate structured self-reflection instrument, not just a well-written quiz.

03 / 09

How the engine works

The eight threads

Every answer nudges eight 0–100 traits, drawn in the UI as moving threads of light rather than a visible scoreboard, so the player feels change without being able to game it.

CodeThreadWhat it measures
AUTSelf-AuthorshipHow much the marriage's meaning depends on having chosen it yourself.
KINKinship PullHow embedded the player is in the family system — and whether that system reads as net or cage.
ANXAnxious WiringFear of abandonment; reads silence as information it usually isn't.
AVOAvoidant WiringDeactivation under closeness; experiences a partner's need as demand.
AMBAmbiguity ToleranceCapacity to hold an unresolved question without needing to close it.
REGRegret OrientationWhether the player regrets action or inaction more, over a lifetime horizon.
PRSInherited PressureThe measured gap between what the player wants and what they believe is expected of their gender (from the Mirror).
RDYReadinessEmotional availability and self-knowledge — deliberately orthogonal to which door fits.

Three doors, not two

The love-vs-arranged framing is itself treated as a false binary: the engine scores a genuine third architecture, the Bridge (family opens the door, the player chooses whether to walk through), with its own weighted formula rather than as an average of the other two.

SEC   = 100 − max(ANX, AVO) + 8
SEC_L = 100 − (0.65·AVO + 0.45·ANX)

love     = 0.30·AUT + 0.20·AMB + 0.20·REG + 0.15·(100−KIN) + 0.15·SEC_L  − 0.14·PRS
arranged = 0.30·KIN + 0.20·(100−AMB) + 0.20·(100−REG) + 0.15·(100−AUT) + 0.15·SEC
bridge   = 0.45·min(AUT,KIN) + 0.25·(100−|AUT−KIN|) + 0.15·AMB + 0.15·SEC − 0.05·PRS

Two choices in this formula are worth flagging as deliberate rather than incidental: insecurity is priced differently per door (avoidance costs the Chosen more; anxiety costs the Given more — a single flat security term would have pushed every insecure profile toward love marriage, which the author judged wrong), and inherited pressure only taxes the doors that require standing against the room — the Given pays nothing, because it's the door the room already approves of. The three raw scores are normalised to percentages, with a confidence label derived from the gap between first and second place: Clear above 14 points, Leaning 6–14, Split below 6 — and a Split result is reported as a genuine finding, not a measurement failure.

The Mirror — measuring pressure instead of asking about it

Six scenarios (who initiates, career vs. relocation, financial asymmetry, emotional expression in conflict, the age "clock," post-marriage caregiving) are each presented as two sliders: what I actually want and what's expected of someone like me. The engine records only the distance between them —

PRS = mean( |want − expected| ) across the six Mirror scenarios

A near-zero gap is reported as a real strength ("aligned — low friction"), regardless of which side it's aligned to; a wide gap is reported as a cost the player is carrying, not an opinion about their gender. This is the project's most original mechanic — it sidesteps the usual failure of "do you believe X" questions, which everyone answers to sound modern and which therefore measure nothing.

The Council — five agents, a router, a synthesiser

A second, independent layer re-reads the same eight traits through five specialists (Attachment, Kinship, Mirror, Regret, Readiness), each returning a relevance score, a leaning door, a confidence, and a stated concern. A router seats only the agents whose relevance clears a threshold; the rest are shown, by name, as consulted but not seated. A synthesiser then resolves one of four outcomes every single reading: the council agrees with the arithmetic, it dissents from it (both are printed, and the arithmetic wins but the dissent is kept as a visible warning), it records internal splits between agents protecting different parts of the player, or Readiness vetoes the whole verdict regardless of door. Nothing here is machine-learned — it is the vocabulary and transparency ethos of a multi-agent system applied to five short deterministic functions — but the effect for the reader is a report that is visibly allowed to disagree with itself.

04 / 09

Data & technical architecture

The entire experience — canvas-animated background, 26-beat narrative engine, scoring, and verdict rendering — is one dependency-free HTML file. There is no build step, no package manager, and no framework; the only external calls are to Google Fonts. This is a genuine strength: the project will still run, unmodified, in ten years, from a single file, offline.

Every completed reading is written to localStorage under the key bridge_records_v1, and — if a Firebase project is configured — mirrored to Cloud Firestore via plain REST calls, no SDK. A companion dashboard (admin.html) reads both sources, de-duplicates by record id, and presents KPI tiles, a verdict-share donut, per-thread population averages split by gender, a readiness histogram against the 45-point gate, and a per-user drill-down that reproduces the full arithmetic for that specific reading — an "explain" view built to prove the score wasn't guessed. Access is gated behind Firebase Authentication with a single hardcoded admin account.

05 / 09

What's genuinely original here

  • Failure-mode framing. Scoring "which failure you can survive" instead of "which you'd prefer" is a real reframe, not a marketing line — it changes what every question in the instrument is trying to elicit.
  • An orthogonal readiness gate. Decoupling which door fits your temperament from are you ready to walk through any door this year is the single most clinically sound design decision in the system, and most comparable tools conflate the two.
  • The Mirror's measured-gap approach. Scoring the distance between want and perceived expectation, instead of asking directly about gender beliefs, is a genuinely clever way to extract signal that a direct question would only let people perform past.
  • The Bridge as a real third architecture. Giving the hybrid option its own formula term — min(AUT, KIN) — rather than treating it as the average of the other two means it can only score high when both needs are genuinely strong, not when someone is lukewarm about both.
  • A second opinion allowed to disagree. The Council's dissent/split/veto/no-quorum outcomes mean the reader sometimes sees the system disagree with itself in public — a rare and honest choice for software that could just as easily print one confident number.
  • Coherence as signal. Tracking contradiction between answers that should co-vary, and reporting it directly ("you are arguing with someone who isn't in the room"), turns noisy data into a finding instead of discarding it.
06 / 09

Findings & remediation

A hands-on pass — running the full 26-beat playthrough programmatically, reading both files end to end, checking the deployed security rules, and later re-testing every change live in a browser — turned up the following. Of eight findings and one shipped feature: seven fixes are in the code now, and one (the delete path) was deliberately left as-is at the owner's explicit request. The Firestore rule fix still needs a manual deploy to take effect on the live project — noted inline below.

Blocking Fixed

admin.html hardcoded the admin sign-in email as ghosuddin.d@gmail.com — missing a letter against the real account, ghousuddin.d@gmail.com. As shipped, admin login would have failed unconditionally. Corrected in place.

Shipped

A "Share your result" action on the verdict screen: a canvas-rendered result card (door, confidence, all three door percentages, top driving threads) styled to match the game's own palette, offered through the native share sheet where available and falling back to a PNG download.

Data exposure Fixed

The deployed Firestore rule was allow create: if true with no field or type validation on the written document — any client on the internet, not only this app, could write arbitrary documents into the readings collection. firestore.rules now requires the document to match the exact shape the app writes (json + ts string fields only, size-capped) and the id to match the app's own id format. This still needs to be deployed to the live Firebase project (console, or firebase deploy --only firestore:rules) before it takes effect — the file is fixed, the running database isn't yet.

Terminology Fixed

admin.html labelled its scoring breakdown "How the AI scored it." There is no AI or machine learning anywhere in the system — it's a fixed, published weighted formula, which is actually a strength (auditable, reproducible). Relabelled to "How the engine scored it" in admin.html, and the matching phrase corrected in design.md for consistency.

Privacy Fixed

Sensitive relational and psychological data was collected with no disclosure in the product itself. The Threshold screen now states plainly, before anyone answers anything, that the reading is saved locally and — if cloud sync is on — to a shared archive only the admin can read, and that nobody, including the person answering, can edit or delete it afterward.

Delete path Kept as-is

The Firestore rules still block every update and delete unconditionally, including for the person a reading belongs to. Raised as a recommendation; the owner asked explicitly to leave this behaviour unchanged. Documented here so the decision is on the record, not lost.

Test coverage Fixed

score(), convene(), and admin's independent explain() had zero automated coverage. A new test/engine.test.mjs (run via npm test, Node's built-in test runner only — no new dependencies) now covers door-share normalisation, which trait profile wins which door, the confidence-band thresholds, the readiness veto, and — the one most worth having — a parity check proving admin's hand-decomposed formula still matches the game's formula exactly. 10 tests, all passing.

Accessibility Fixed

Three real gaps closed: --dim text was ~3.2:1 contrast against the background (fails WCAG AA) and is now ~4.9:1; the ambient particle field and background thread-braid ran continuously regardless of prefers-reduced-motion and now freeze under it; and the range sliders plus the radar chart had no ARIA labelling and now do. One more turned up along the way, below.

New — keyboard blocker Fixed

Not in the original pass: the gender/age "chip" selectors on the Threshold screen were plain, unfocusable <div>s wired to onclick only. A keyboard-only visitor could never select either one — and both are required before "Begin" enables — which meant the entire game was unreachable without a mouse. Chips are now real, focusable controls (role="button", tabindex, Enter/Space handling, aria-pressed state), verified end-to-end with simulated keyboard input.

07 / 09

Limitations & risks

  • No outcome validation. The formula's weights are hand-authored and checked against a handful of simulated archetype playthroughs, not fit to real relationship outcomes or benchmarked against a peer-reviewed instrument (e.g. the ECR-R attachment scale, which the ANX/AVO threads clearly draw on). Still true — this needs real data or a study, not code.
  • Self-report, scenario-based measurement. Every trait is inferred from a hypothetical choice, not observed behaviour — a well-known ceiling on any instrument of this kind, psychometric or not. Structural; not fixable by this project alone.
  • One cultural frame. The scenarios, the family-pressure content, and the love/arranged binary itself are written from a South Asian / diaspora vantage point. The engine's math is culture-agnostic, but the copy is not, and the tool doesn't yet generalise to contexts with different marriage norms. Still true — needs a content decision from the owner.
  • No delete path. The Firestore rules block updates and deletes unconditionally, including for the person a reading belongs to. Raised and consciously kept as-is at the owner's request; the consent half of this — no privacy notice — is now fixed (see §6).
  • No automated test coverage. Resolved — score(), convene(), and admin's explain() are now covered by test/engine.test.mjs. A test of this kind would not have caught the admin email typo specifically (that lived in a login flow, not the scoring engine) — but the engine itself is no longer unverified.
  • Partial accessibility coverage. Improved — the three gaps named in the original review (reduced-motion, slider/chart labelling, text contrast) are fixed, plus one found along the way (keyboard-unreachable chips). Not a full audit: color contrast was checked only for --dim/--mut, and no screen-reader was run end-to-end against the verdict report's dense data tables.

09 / 09

Conclusion

Impulse is unusually thoughtful software for its genre. It refuses easy flattery, models a real third option that most comparable tools ignore entirely, separates readiness from fit where almost nothing else in this space does, and explains its own reasoning — including where that reasoning disagrees with itself — instead of hiding behind a single confident number. Built with zero dependencies in a form that will still run untouched in a decade, it is also, quietly, a well-made piece of software.

Its original gap wasn't ambition — it was trust-surface hardening, and that part is now largely closed: the open write rule has field validation, the "AI" mislabel is corrected, the Threshold screen discloses what it collects, the scoring engine has real test coverage including a cross-file parity check, and a keyboard-accessibility blocker that would have locked out an entire class of visitors is fixed. One integrity item — a self-serve delete path — was raised and deliberately left as-is at the owner's request; that's a decision on the record, not an oversight.

What's left is exactly what code can't supply on its own: real evidence that the scoring weights track real outcomes, and a decision about whether — and how — to speak to an audience beyond the one the current scenarios were written for. Both are the owner's calls to make. Everything downstream of those two decisions — the comparison mode, the print layout, engine versioning — is straightforward once they're settled.
