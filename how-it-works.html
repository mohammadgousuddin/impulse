Full Project Report

How Impulse works

A chapter-by-chapter walkthrough of the whole system — the game, the scoring engine, and the dashboard — in the same order a player actually experiences it.

Subject: github.com/mohammadgousuddin/impulse Companion to: report.html (findings & recommendations) Written: 25 August 2026

Impulse is one continuous system built from three moving parts: a 26-beat narrative that quietly moves eight numeric traits as you answer, a scoring engine that turns those eight numbers into a verdict and a five-agent second opinion, and an archive + dashboard that remembers every reading anyone takes. This report walks through all three in the order a player meets them — chapter by chapter — then closes with how the pieces underneath actually connect.

One thing runs underneath every chapter and isn't a chapter itself: a full-screen canvas animation of two threads of light — one rose, one violet — drifting far apart at the start and slowly braiding toward the centre as the progress bar fills. Each chapter also re-tints the whole background (the "mood") so House feels warm and amber, the Room With No Lights feels cold and distant, the Mirror feels pale and detached, and so on. None of this touches the scoring — it's pure atmosphere, driven by a MOODS table and a requestAnimationFrame loop — but it's how the player feels the eight numbers changing without ever seeing a number. It also respects prefers-reduced-motion: the particle drift and the thread's wave-crawl both freeze for anyone who has that preference set, while the mood colour transitions (a meaningful state change, not ambient motion) still happen, just more gently.

0
Chapter Zero

The Threshold

"Before anyone asks who you will marry, someone already taught you what a marriage is." — this screen, though, asks nothing narrative yet. It's the only screen in the whole game that's pure setup.
Mood
intro
Inputs
4 fields
Threads touched
KIN (indirectly)
Function
scrThreshold()

Four things are collected here, and only one of them feeds the engine directly:

  • A name — "what should this reading call you," free text, used only to personalise the report and archive.
  • Gender — three chips (A man / A woman / Neither / other). This never scores anything. Its only job is deciding whose expectation the Mirror (Chapter IV) asks about — "what's expected of a man" vs "a woman" vs "someone like you."
  • Age band — five chips, recorded for the archive and dashboard, not scored.
  • Family volume — a single slider, "a distant radio" to "a voice in the room." This one does score: the moment you press Begin, startPlay() runs S.T.KIN = clamp(S.T.KIN + (familyVolume−50)×0.30) — a first, quiet nudge to Kinship Pull before a single narrative question has been asked.

Both chip groups are keyboard-operable (arrow to a chip, Enter or Space to select) — worth noting because, in the shipped version, they weren't: a plain unfocusable <div> with no tabindex meant a keyboard-only visitor could never select a gender or age and could never reach the "Begin" button at all. Fixed since. The screen also now states, before anyone answers anything, what's collected and that nobody — including the person answering — can edit or delete it afterward.

"Begin" stays disabled until name, gender, and age are all set. Pressing it calls startPlay(), which applies the family-volume nudge, turns on the left-edge thread rail (the eight threads of light that track your traits for the rest of the game, unlabelled), and moves to the Chapter I interstitial.

I
Chapter One

The House You Grew Up In

"Before anyone asks who you will marry, someone already taught you what a marriage is. Usually without saying a word. Walk back through that house. The lights are still on."
Mood
house — warm amber
Scenarios
5
Primary thread
KIN
Secondary
AUT, RDY, AVO, ANX

Every scenario in this chapter is about the family system: is it a net or a cage, and what does it cost to disagree with it out loud. A representative beat —

"You are nineteen. You've made a decision your parents disagree with — nothing dramatic. A course. A city. Nobody shouts. Your mother just goes quiet." What do you do?
→ "The silence works. I fold before dessert." (Kinship rises, Self-Authorship and Readiness fall)
→ "I hold, and I let her carry it." (Kinship falls, Self-Authorship rises)

The other four scenarios cover: what the house becomes when something goes wrong for you; what actually moves in your chest watching a cousin's arranged match unfold; how fast disapproval would cost you if you married against the family's wishes; and what you do when you're provably right about something your parents are wrong about. Each choice nudges KIN up or down, and several also touch AUT, RDY, or the hidden coherence signal — deliberately, since a chapter about family pressure is also the first place contradictions between "what I want" and "what I was taught to want" start to show.

II
Chapter Two

The Room With No Lights

"This chapter is not about romance. It is about what you do when the person you love goes quiet, and you cannot see their face, and the only voice left in the room is yours."
Mood
dark — cold, distant blue
Scenarios
5
Primary threads
ANX, AVO
Secondary
RDY

This is the attachment-style chapter, and it's the most psychometrically direct one — five scenarios that map almost one-to-one onto anxious vs. avoidant attachment research: silence after a message, the moment right after a real fight, being needed a great deal, six calm months with no drama, and a hurt you've never told anyone.

"Someone you love hasn't replied in nine hours. Nothing is wrong. Nine hours."
→ "I've built three explanations and two of them end with me alone." (Anxious Wiring rises sharply, Readiness falls)
→ "I noticed. And I let it be nine hours." (Anxious Wiring falls, Readiness rises)

Anxious Wiring (ANX) and Avoidant Wiring (AVO) move independently here — a player can push both up (the "fearful" combination the Council's Attachment agent treats as its own case, described in Chapter VI), or neither. These two numbers end up doing real work later: they define SEC and SEC_L, the security terms that price insecurity differently against each door.

III
Chapter Three

The Fork

"Two doors. One you open. One is opened for you. The difference is not comfort — it is who holds the story afterwards, and who you get to blame when the story turns."
Mood
fork — rose vs. teal, split
Scenarios
5
Primary threads
AUT, AMB
Secondary
KIN, REG, coherence

Where Chapter I asked "how loud is the room," this chapter asks "how much do you need to have chosen it yourself, and how much unresolved not-knowing can you actually tolerate." The mood itself literally splits — rose light from one side, teal from the other — to match the fork metaphor.

"A sealed envelope on the left. An open horizon on the right. Whichever you touch, the other one turns to smoke — and you will remember the smoke." Certainty now, or the possibility of something better later?
→ "Certainty. I know what uncertainty does to me." (Ambiguity Tolerance falls)
→ "Possibility. I'd rather ache than settle." (Ambiguity Tolerance and Regret both rise)

The other four scenarios: choosing between a harder self-authored life and an easier given one; six months into a relationship with no certainty either way; being handed a vetted shortlist by family; and what actually makes a marriage feel like yours. This is where AUT (Self-Authorship) and AMB (Ambiguity Tolerance) — the two traits that directly define the Bridge door's formula — get most of their signal.

IV
Chapter Four

The Mirror

"Here you answer everything twice. Once as yourself. Once as the person the world expects you to be. The distance between those two answers is the most honest number in this whole reading."
Mood
mirror — pale, detached lavender
Scenarios
6
Produces
PRS
Mechanic
dual sliders, not choices

This is the one chapter that isn't multiple-choice. Each of the six scenarios shows two sliders side by side over the same 0–100 scale: "What I actually want" and "What's expected of [a man / a woman / someone like you]" — the second label pulled from the gender picked back in the Threshold. The engine never puts gendered content into either slider itself; the player supplies both ends, and the engine only ever measures the gap between them.

"Two people at the edge of becoming something. One of them has to say it first." Who moves first? Who says the thing that cannot be taken back?
Slider A (want): "They should" ←――――○――――→ "I should"
Slider B (expected): "They should" ←――――――――○→ "I should"
A wide gap between the two dots here is recorded — not judged.

The six topics: who initiates, career vs. relocation, financial asymmetry, how much feeling is allowed to show in a fight, how much the age "clock" presses on the decision, and whose parents get cared for after the wedding. After all six, the engine computes:

PRS = mean( |want − expected| ) across the six Mirror scenarios
S.T.PRS = clamp(PRS × 1.55)
S.coh   = clamp(S.coh − S.T.PRS × 0.28)   // wide gaps also cost coherence

A near-zero gap is reported later as a genuine strength — "aligned, low friction" — regardless of which side it's aligned to. A wide gap becomes PRS (Inherited Pressure), the one thread that taxes the Chosen and Bridge doors directly in the final scoring, and the input the Mirror agent in the Council reads most closely.

V
Chapter Five

Ten Years From Now

"You are older now. The choice is long made; the wedding photographs have started to look like photographs of other people. These are not questions about the future. They are questions about what you can forgive yourself for."
Mood
dawn — peach, coral
Scenarios
5
Primary thread
REG
Secondary
RDY, AUT, KIN, coherence

Written entirely in second-person future tense — "you are alone in a parked car," "they are sixteen and furious" — this is the only chapter that asks the player to read their own older self. It measures REG (Regret Orientation): whether, over a lifetime, you'd forgive yourself faster for a wrong action or a missed one — the single strongest predictor in the whole system of whether someone can live with having chosen love.

"Last one. Empty hall. No audience, no aunties, no group chat, no God watching through anyone else's eyes. Just you and the choice." If no one would ever know or judge your choice, does the choice change?
→ "No. Same answer." (Readiness and coherence both rise)
→ "Yes. Completely." (Readiness and coherence fall — and this specifically sets an external flag the verdict calls out by name)

That last question is the only place in the game with a direct side effect beyond the eight threads: answering that the choice would change sets S.external = true, which adds a specific closing card to the final report — "you said the choice would change if nobody were watching."

VI
The Verdict

Where the two threads meet

Visually, this is the payoff the whole game has been building toward: braid hits 1 (the rose and violet threads fully interweave at screen centre) and heart hits 1 (a glow blooms where they meet). Mechanically, five things happen in sequence inside finish().
  1. score() — turns the eight threads into three door percentages and a confidence label.
  2. failure(top) — picks the one specific way this profile most likely fails inside its winning door.
  3. theAsk(top) — generates up to four findable qualities to look for in a partner, from whichever threads read extreme.
  4. convene(r) — runs the Council: five agents re-read the same eight numbers independently and a synthesiser decides whether they agree with the arithmetic.
  5. saveRecord(r,f,C) — archives everything to localStorage, and to the cloud if configured.

The three doors

SEC   = 100 − max(ANX, AVO) + 8
SEC_L = 100 − (0.65·AVO + 0.45·ANX)   // avoidance-weighted, used only by love

love     = 0.30·AUT + 0.20·AMB + 0.20·REG + 0.15·(100−KIN) + 0.15·SEC_L  − 0.14·PRS
arranged = 0.30·KIN + 0.20·(100−AMB) + 0.20·(100−REG) + 0.15·(100−AUT) + 0.15·SEC
bridge   = 0.45·min(AUT,KIN) + 0.25·(100−|AUT−KIN|) + 0.15·AMB + 0.15·SEC − 0.05·PRS

The three raw numbers are normalised to percentages of each other. The gap between first and second place sets the confidence label: Clear above 14 points, Leaning 6–14, Split below 6 (reported as a real finding, not a failed measurement). failure(top) then checks the winning door against specific thread thresholds — love with high ANX becomes "Disillusionment through intensity," arranged with high AUT becomes "The quiet resignation," bridge with low AMB becomes "Paralysis at the bridge," and so on — seven named failure modes in total, plus a fallback ("The drift") naming the runner-up door as where the player is most likely to drift under stress.

The Council — a second opinion allowed to disagree

Five specialist functions re-read the same eight numbers, each blind to the others' conclusions, each returning a relevance (how much this player's answers actually give it to weigh), a leaning door, a confidence, a finding, and a stated concern:

Attachment — reads Chapter II. Anxious → leans Bridge (needs two guarantees). Avoidant → leans Given. Both high → abstains and raises an alarm instead.
Kinship — reads Chapter I. High kinship + high autonomy → Bridge. High kinship alone → Given. Low kinship → Chosen.
Mirror — reads Chapter IV. Loud inherited script + strong will → Bridge ("love isn't wrong for you, it's expensive"). Loud script, weak will → Given.
Regret — reads Chapter V. Regrets inaction more → Chosen. Regrets action more → Given.
Readiness — reads everything. Never leans toward a door. Holds the one hard veto in the system.

A router seats only the agents whose relevance clears 45; the rest appear, by name, as "consulted but not seated." A synthesiser then resolves one outcome, checked in this order:

PriorityOutcomeWhat the reader sees
1VetoReadiness reads below 45 — the door still shows, but the report opens with "not this year," overriding everything below it.
2DissentThe Council's weighted vote picks a different door than the arithmetic. Both numbers are printed; the arithmetic still wins, but the disagreement is kept visible as a warning.
3No quorumNo seated agent found its domain loud enough to argue for a door. Reported as a real, fairly enviable result — nothing in the profile is fighting itself.
4SplitAgents agree on the door but disagree underneath it — two of them wanted different things for the player. Named, with what each one was protecting.
5AgreementEvery seated agent points the same way as the arithmetic. Rare, and flagged as such — the risk stops being which door and becomes whether you'll actually walk through it.

The report itself

Rendered top to bottom: the door name and confidence; a bar for each of the three doors ranked; a radar chart of all eight threads (with a screen-reader label summarising every value, added in the recent accessibility pass); "the eight threads" with a plain-language note per thread; "why" — the top three threads that actually decided it; the full Council panel; the failure mode; "your ask" — what to look for in a partner; "the invoice" — the stated price of the winning door; readiness, called out separately; a Mirror card if inherited pressure read above 40; and the "one more thing" card if the external flag was set in Chapter V.

Three actions close the report: Save this reading (window.print()), Begin again (reload), and Share your result — the newest feature, added this engagement. It draws a portrait-format canvas card (door, confidence, all three door percentages, the top driving threads) styled to match the game's own palette, then hands it to the native share sheet where available or falls back to a PNG download.

A
Appendix

The admin dashboard

admin.html is a separate surface entirely — nobody playing the game ever sees it. It exists for one person (the project owner) to watch the archive grow.

  1. Gate — a login screen mapping a friendly typed user id ("impulse") to the real admin email, verified against Firebase Authentication. The session lives in sessionStorage and ends when the tab closes.
  2. Overview — KPI tiles (total readings, split verdicts, readiness average, pressure average, Council dissent rate), a verdict-share donut, per-thread population averages split by gender when both are present, and a readiness histogram against the 45-point gate.
  3. The table — every reading, sortable and searchable, each row showing name, date, a mini thread sparkline, predicted door, score, and confidence.
  4. The drawer — clicking a row expands the full record: all eight threads, all three door scores, the entire Council breakdown for that specific reading, every Mirror gap, and a term-by-term arithmetic table proving exactly how the score was computed (mirrors §C below, run against that user's real numbers).
  5. Tools — Refresh (re-pull from the cloud), Export/Import JSON (move the archive between devices), Seed demo data (14 generated profiles run through the real engine, for exploring the dashboard before real data exists), and Clear all (local-only; cloud records are untouched).
B
Appendix

Data, storage & security

Two layers, always. Every completed reading is written to localStorage under bridge_records_v1 the instant the verdict renders — this is why the game works offline and a reading is never lost to a bad connection. If a Firebase project is configured, the same record is also POSTed to Cloud Firestore over plain REST (no SDK) as two fields: a JSON string and a timestamp.

The deployed firestore.rules allow anyone to create a reading, allow only the authenticated admin to read the archive, and block every update and delete unconditionally — including for the person a reading belongs to, a deliberate choice the owner has asked to keep as-is. The create rule now validates the document's shape (only the two expected string fields, size-capped, id format checked) rather than accepting any arbitrary write, closing what was previously an open write target for any client on the internet.
